# History research: early modern Europe and East China

Reference material for card design: the buildings, materials and technologies of roughly 1450–1900,
the span `framework.md` describes as "from the late medieval period to late industrialization". This
file records history, not design decisions — nothing here is settled for the game until it lands in a
framework file.

Mostly compiled from general historical knowledge; the less familiar Chinese facts were spot-checked
against the sources at the end. Dates marked "c." are approximate.

The four-era split below is a proposal for organising the material, not a project decision —
`framework.md` only says "3–4 eras".

---

## Europe

### Era 1: Renaissance and gunpowder (c. 1450–1600)

**Materials**
- **Saltpetre:** the hard ingredient of gunpowder. Grown in "nitre beds" of dung, urine and earth;
  crown "saltpetre men" had the right to dig up people's barn floors. Later imported from Bengal.
- **Sulphur** (mostly from Sicily) and **charcoal**. Charcoal eats forests, and that wood shortage
  later pushes Europe into coal.
- **Copper and tin:** together they make bronze, the first cannon metal. Bell-founders became
  gun-founders.
- **Silver:** first from Central European mines, which made the Fugger bankers rich; from 1545 from
  Potosí in South America, which flooded Europe.
- **Mercury:** needed to refine silver.
- **Alum:** fixes cloth dyes; under a papal monopoly.
- **Naval stores:** timber, hemp, tar and pitch from the Baltic.

**Buildings**
- Powder mill
- Gun foundry
- Blast furnace: produces cast iron. England's first cast-iron cannon was made in 1543.
- **Star fort** ("trace italienne"): low, angled walls built because cannon smashed medieval walls —
  the classic Defense answer to an Attack technology.
- Printing house (Gutenberg, c. 1450)
- Venetian Arsenal: built war galleys in a near-assembly-line process.
- Mine with water-wheel pumps
- Bank or counting house (Medici, Fugger)
- University, botanical garden (Padua 1545), observatory (Tycho's Uraniborg 1576)

**Technologies**
- Corned powder (more reliable gunpowder)
- Matchlock, then arquebus and musket
- Pike-and-shot formations
- Ships: caravel, carrack, galleon
- Navigation: compass, astrolabe, Mercator map (1569)
- Double-entry bookkeeping (1494)
- Heliocentrism and modern anatomy (both 1543)

### Era 2: Commerce and the Scientific Revolution (c. 1600–1750)

**Materials**
- Colonial cash crops: sugar, tobacco, coffee, tea, cocoa
- Indian cotton cloth (calico), indigo, furs, cod
- **Whale oil** for lamps
- **Coal:** England ran short of wood and burned coal for heat, brewing, glass and salt.
- New World food crops: potatoes and maize
- Quinine bark

**Buildings**
- Joint-stock company (English East India Company 1600, Dutch VOC 1602)
- Stock exchange (Amsterdam 1602)
- Central bank (Amsterdam 1609, England 1694)
- **Coffeehouse:** where news, deals and insurance happened; Lloyd's of London started in one.
- Windmill and polder (Dutch land reclamation)
- Royal academy (Royal Society 1660, French Academy 1666)
- Royal observatory
- Canal (Canal du Midi 1681)
- Sugar refinery, glassworks, ropewalk
- Barracks for standing armies
- **Porcelain manufactory:** Meissen (1710) finally copied the Chinese secret.

**Technologies**
- Telescope (1609), microscope, pendulum clock (1656)
- **Barometer and vacuum pump** (1640s–50s): the physics that leads straight to steam
- Logarithms, calculus, Newton's physics (1687)
- **Flintlock and bayonet:** these make the pike obsolete.
- Dutch fluyt, a cheap cargo ship
- Turnip-and-clover crop rotation; Tull's seed drill (1701)
- Smallpox inoculation (1721, learned from Ottoman practice)
- Newspapers and patent law
- **First steam engines:** Savery 1698, Newcomen 1712
- **Coke smelting of iron:** Darby, 1709

### Era 3: First Industrial Revolution (c. 1750–1840)

**Materials**
- **Coal** and **coke**
- Wrought iron
- **Raw cotton** (from the American South after the cotton gin, 1793)
- Industrial chemicals: sulphuric acid, soda (1791), chlorine bleach
- Town gas

**Buildings**
- **Cotton mill:** Arkwright's Cromford, 1771 — water-powered, usually called the first true factory.
- Ironworks: Coalbrookdale, where the Iron Bridge was built in 1779
- Coal mine with steam pump
- Canal: the Bridgewater Canal (1761) halved the price of coal in Manchester.
- Pottery works: Wedgwood's Etruria, built on division of labour
- Engine works: Boulton & Watt
- Machine shop: Portsmouth block mills (1803), the first mass production with machine tools
- Chemical works
- Gasworks: London street lighting from 1812
- **Railway:** Stockton–Darlington 1825, Liverpool–Manchester 1830

**Technologies**
- Textile machines in order: flying shuttle (1733), spinning jenny, water frame, spinning mule, power
  loom (1785)
- **Watt engine:** a separate condenser (1769) and later rotary motion
- **Precision cylinder boring** (1774): developed for cannon, and it made Watt's cylinders possible
- **Puddling and rolling** (1784): mass-produced wrought iron
- Crucible steel
- High-pressure steam, the locomotive and the steamship (1807)
- Interchangeable parts
- **Jacquard loom** (1804): controlled by punched cards
- Vaccination (1796), the battery (1800), modern chemistry (Lavoisier)
- Optical telegraph (1794), macadam roads

### Era 4: Second Industrial Revolution (c. 1840–1900)

**Materials**
- **Steel**, now cheap
- **Oil and kerosene**
- **Rubber** (vulcanized from 1839)
- **Guano and Chilean nitrate:** saltpetre again, now for fertiliser and explosives — a bookend to the
  gunpowder start.
- Coal tar, copper for wiring, aluminium (1886)

**Buildings**
- **Steelworks:** Bessemer process 1856, open hearth 1860s, Krupp at Essen
- **Oil well and refinery:** Łukasiewicz's kerosene lamp (1853) and oil field (1854) in Polish
  Galicia, Drake's well in Pennsylvania (1859), Baku with the Nobel brothers (1870s)
- Telegraph office (transatlantic cable 1866)
- Dye works (synthetic mauve 1856; BASF and Bayer)
- Dynamite factory (1867)
- **Power station** (1882)
- **Research laboratory:** Edison's Menlo Park (1876), where invention itself became an industry
- Hospital (germ theory, antiseptics)
- Arms works: rifled breech-loaders, the Maxim gun (1884)

**Technologies**
- Dynamo, electric light (1879), telephone (1876)
- Internal combustion: Otto 1876, Benz car 1886, Diesel 1890s
- Synthetic chemistry
- Anaesthesia (1846) and antisepsis
- Photography, refrigeration
- Haber–Bosch ammonia (1909–13): the edge of the scope

### Chains worth building cards around

Real historical dependencies, so they make natural card combos:

- **Gunpowder:** saltpetre + sulphur + charcoal, then powder mill, then foundry, then cannon. That
  leads to the star fort as the counter, and to cannon-armed ships.
- **Steam was invented to pump water out of coal mines.** Coal mine + pump engine is the founding
  combo. Watt's engine then needed cannon-boring precision, so a military technology unlocks an
  industrial one.
- **Iron:** charcoal furnace, then coke (Darby), then puddling, then Bessemer steel, then railways and
  steel ships. Each step removes a bottleneck: first wood, then quality, then price.
- **Textiles:** imported calico creates demand, then spinning machines, then the power loom. Mass
  production then needs bleach and dyes, so the textile chain pulls in the chemical chain.
- **Light and fuel:** whale oil, then town gas, then kerosene, then petroleum, then the engine.

## East China (Ming 1368–1644, Qing 1644–1912)

China's economic heartland was **Jiangnan**: Suzhou, Hangzhou, Nanjing, Songjiang (today's Shanghai)
and Yangzhou. To that add the **Fujian** coast, **Jingdezhen** for porcelain, and **Canton and Foshan**
in the south.

### Early Ming (c. 1368–1500)
- **Grand Canal restored** (1411–15): it fed the north with Jiangnan rice.
- Forbidden City (1406–20)
- **Zheng He's treasure fleets** (1405–33), built at Nanjing's shipyard. China then turned inward under
  the sea ban.
- **Shenjiying:** a dedicated firearms division under the Yongle emperor (c. 1409).
- A military manual already describing rockets, land mines and naval mines
- Yongle Encyclopedia (1408)
- Imperial porcelain kilns at Jingdezhen
- **Ever-normal granaries:** state grain stores that bought cheap and sold dear to smooth famines

### Late Ming commercial boom (c. 1500–1644)
- **Silver flood:** from Japan, and from the Americas via the Manila galleons (from 1571). The "Single
  Whip" reform (1580s) made taxes payable in silver.
- Trade port Yuegang, Fujian, opened in 1567
- **Songjiang cotton cloth:** the leading cotton centre, mostly spun and woven at home. **Suzhou silk.**
- **Jingdezhen export porcelain:** blue-and-white needs cobalt imported from Persia, and kaolin clay is
  named after the nearby village of Gaoling.
- **Foshan iron:** cast-iron woks and pans
- Merchant networks from Huizhou and Shanxi, with guild halls in every city
- **Commercial woodblock printing**, private libraries (Tianyi Ge 1561), academies
- **Folangji cannon:** breech-loaders copied from the Portuguese (1520s)
- **Hongyipao** ("red barbarian cannon"): copied from Dutch and English guns, decisive at Ningyuan
  (1626)
- General Qi Jiguang's anti-pirate army; the Great Wall rebuilt in brick
- **Tiangong Kaiwu** (1637): an illustrated encyclopedia of all crafts — a natural big technology card.
- Bencao Gangmu (materia medica), Xu Guangqi's agricultural compendium, Euclid translated with the
  Jesuit Matteo Ricci (1607)
- **New World crops:** sweet potato (Fujian, 1590s), maize, peanuts, tobacco

### High Qing (1644–1800)
- **Population boom:** roughly doubles to 300 million+, driven by the new crops and the opening of hill
  land
- **Canton System** (1757–1842): all Western trade went through one port and a licensed merchant guild.
- **Tea export boom** (Fujian, Anhui)
- Jesuit astronomers at the Imperial Observatory; Verbiest also cast cannon for the Kangxi emperor.
- **Salt monopoly:** the Yangzhou salt merchants were among the richest people in the world.
- **Shanxi banks** (piaohao): remittance banks, from 1823
- **Zigong brine and gas wells** (Sichuan, western China but worth taking): percussion-drilled wells.
  The Shenhai well (1835) reached about 1,000 m. The natural gas was piped through bamboo to boil brine
  into salt — China's "oil" card, 25 years before Pennsylvania.
- Smallpox inoculation (by the 1500s, ahead of Europe) and the mulberry–fish-pond farming loop in the
  Pearl River delta
- The huge Siku Quanshu library project (1773–82)

### Late Qing: crisis and Self-Strengthening (1839–1911)
- Opium and the Opium Wars (1839–42, 1856–60); treaty ports and the rise of Shanghai
- **Self-Strengthening Movement:** state factories copying Western industry:
  - Jiangnan Arsenal, Shanghai (1865), which included a translation bureau
  - Fuzhou Navy Yard (1866)
  - China Merchants Steam Navigation (1872)
  - Kaiping coal mines (1878), with a railway
  - Telegraph (1881)
  - Hanyang Iron Works (1890s)
  - The Beiyang fleet, destroyed by Japan in 1894–95

### How China could play differently

Design ideas drawn from the history; none of them is decided.

- **Head start, flatter curve.** China had gunpowder, the compass, printing, paper and cast iron long
  before Europe. Its early-era cards would be strong, but it mechanizes late. The Self-Strengthening
  arsenals copied Western designs, so one possible mechanic is cheaper copies of a technology another
  player already owns.
- **Food and Population over machines.** Double-cropping, terraces, chain pumps, new crops and fish
  ponds — what historians call a labour-intensive path (the "industrious revolution"). Europe turns
  Production into machines; China turns Food into Population and craft output.
- **The state as an engine.** Canal, granaries, salt monopoly, imperial kilns, the examination system.
  These suggest buildings that give Knowledge and Defense and grow stronger the more state buildings
  you own. Europe's counterparts are competitive: banks, joint-stock companies, stock exchanges.
- **Luxury exports pull in silver.** Porcelain, silk and tea brought the world's silver to China.
- **Coal in the wrong place.** The historian Kenneth Pomeranz's "Great Divergence" argument is that
  Jiangnan matched England until about 1800, and England pulled ahead because its coal sat next to its
  industry and because it had colonies, while China's coal was far away in the north. A card hook:
  Chinese coal needs a transport card first. This is a debated scholarly argument, not settled fact.
- **Military.** Walls, massed firearms, rockets and anti-piracy forces lean China toward Defense.
  Europe's arms race among rival states and its cannon-armed ships lean it toward Attack and reach.

### Things that crossed between the two

Candidates for shared cards with different art or effects per side: porcelain (Meissen copying
Jingdezhen), tea, silver, inoculation, Jesuit astronomy and cannon, Portuguese guns becoming the
Folangji, and opium.

## Fit with the framework

- **Strategic resource candidates.** `framework.md` N6: "A strategic resource never has more than 1–2
  copies in the game." Candidates:
  - Europe: Saltpetre, Sulphur, Coal, Iron, Silver, Mercury, Copper, Tin, Cotton, Oil, Rubber, Nitrate
  - China: Kaolin, Cobalt blue, Silk, Tea, Salt, Silver (imported, itself a twist), Natural gas
- **Two contrasting civilizations.** `framework.md` open question 9 defaults to "begin with 2
  contrasting civs in the prototype, sharing the same base starter deck plus a few unique cards each."
  Europe and China fit that well, since the history itself gives them different engines.
- **Handle with care.** Plantation sugar, cotton and tobacco relied on enslaved labour, and opium was
  forced on China by war. Ignoring them or making them cheerful bonuses would land badly.
